# The Holy Seed Mingled — Foreign Contact Defiles

> *For they have taken of their daughters for themselves, and for their sons: so that the holy seed have mingled themselves with the people of those lands; yea, the hand of the princes and rulers hath been chief in this trespass.*  
> — Ezra 9:1-2, 10:2-3 (KJV)

| **Summary** | Returned exiles who intermarried with the peoples of the surrounding lands are held to have defiled 'the holy seed' by contact itself; Ezra leads a covenant to 'put away' the foreign wives and their children as the remedy. |
| **Original Audience** | The returned Judean exiles under Persian rule, rebuilding a post-exilic identity anxious about assimilation. |
| **Immediate Context** | Ezra 9-10, the crisis that follows Ezra's arrival in Jerusalem: leaders report widespread intermarriage 'according to their abominations' with neighboring peoples; the community weeps, confesses, and covenants (led by Shechaniah, Ezra 10:2-3) to expel the foreign wives and the children born of them. |
| **Historical Context** | This is the harshest endogamy enforcement in the canon — not a prohibition on future marriage (Deut 7:3) but a retroactive dissolution of existing families and expulsion of children already born. Scholars read it as an anxious minority community's survival response under empire, not a timeless universal. Nehemiah 13:23-27 repeats the crisis a generation later, showing it did not hold. The premise — that a person becomes defiled by sustained proximity/union with 'the nations,' independent of any act of apostasy — is the seed of a contact-based (not conduct-based) theory of impurity, which Haggai 2:11-13 states as an explicit legal principle (uncleanness is 'catching,' holiness is not). |
| **Penalty** | Expulsion of the foreign spouse and the children of the union ('put away all the wives, and such as are born of them'). |
| **Exceptions** | None offered in the text; the episode is presented as covenant renewal, not as one option among several. |
| **Cross References** | • Deuteronomy 7:3<br>• Nehemiah 13:23-27<br>• Haggai 2:11-13<br>• Malachi 2:11 |
| **Related NT Passages** | • Ruth (entire book) — the counter-narrative: a Moabite convert becomes David's great-grandmother, the canon's own rebuttal to contact-based exclusion<br>• Acts 10 — Peter and Cornelius: 'God is no respecter of persons,' table-fellowship across the Gentile line reopened<br>• Galatians 3:28 — 'neither Jew nor Greek... ye are all one in Christ Jesus' |
| **Internal Contradictions** | • Ezra expels foreign wives and their children as defiling the holy seed by contact alone, while the book of Ruth — canonized beside it — makes a Moabite convert the ancestress of David and, in the New Testament's own genealogy, of Christ. The Bible contains both the purge and its refutation. [Ruth 4:13-17, Matthew 1:5] |
| **SAB Categories** | — |
| **USofC Implementation** | The scriptural foundation of the **Returning Citizen doctrine** (see `StateDoctrine.md`): any citizen who has resided among 'the heathen nations' (in practice, any non-covenant jurisdiction — the west-coast Tribes and, more so, actual foreign nations) is presumptively contaminated by contact, independent of any specific act of apostasy. Reinstatement requires either (a) a purification rite and formal renunciation of any foreign attachment formed there, or (b) indefinite residence in a Returning Citizen holding zone ('limbo'), pending a tribunal finding. A citizen who refuses purification and declines to renounce forfeits reinstatement and is treated as having self-exiled. |
| **Agency** | Department of Righteousness (DOR) |
| **Constitutional Analogue** | No real analogue in ordinary immigration/citizenship law; nearest real-world parallel is loyalty-oath denaturalization regimes and quarantine law applied to a person's *relationships* rather than their body. |
| **Scene Ideas** | • A young man returned from the 'bastion of sin' standing before a tribunal that cannot accuse him of any specific sin — only of having been there, among them, long enough.<br>• A clerk reading the Ruth genealogy aloud in defense of a mixed marriage, and the tribunal chairman closing the book before she reaches 'and Boaz begat Obed.'<br>• A choice offered without any good option: purify and renounce, or remain in the holding zone indefinitely, or leave for good. |
| **Satirical Uses** | • The state that requires purification from contact with 'the nations' reads Ruth in church every year and has never once let the moral of the story reach the tribunal room. |
| **Bureaucratic Uses** | • Returning Citizen tribunal intake<br>• Renunciation-and-Purification certificates<br>• indefinite holding-zone residency permits |
